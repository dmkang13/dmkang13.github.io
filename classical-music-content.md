## slide-4 — Early & Medieval

### label
Early & Medieval

### kicker
c. 500–1400

### title
Early &
Medieval

### tracks
Pérotin | Viderunt omnes | 0:12 - 0:42 | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q2JvIyStzNA | 12 | 42

### note
Monophony · Polyphony · Church modes not scales

### speaker-notes
- **The era is vast, and the surviving record is selective:** “Medieval music” covers roughly nine centuries, but most written sources come from monasteries, cathedrals, and courts that had trained scribes and libraries. Sacred music therefore dominates what survives even though everyday life also included dance music, work songs, love songs, and oral storytelling.
- **Plainchant is a communal, text-led tradition:** A single melodic line follows the rhythm of sacred words rather than a regular beat. Its modal organization lacks the strong major-minor pull familiar to modern ears, which can make the music feel suspended, circular, and shaped by contemplation rather than harmonic arrival.
- **Notation transformed memory into a compositional tool:** Neumes first served as reminders for melodies already known by ear; increasingly precise staff notation, associated especially with Guido of Arezzo, made music easier to teach and preserve. Once pitch and rhythm could be represented more clearly, composers could coordinate larger and more complex designs.
- **Polyphony turned chant into musical architecture:** Early organum added voices to a familiar chant, and Notre-Dame composers such as Léonin and Pérotin developed several rhythmically active lines over a slow foundation. Cathedral reverberation blended those layers, making the building itself part of the musical technology.
- **Medieval musical life extended beyond anonymous churchmen:** Troubadours and trouvères wrote secular songs about love, politics, devotion, and satire, while Hildegard of Bingen created sacred works of striking range and imagery. By the fourteenth-century *Ars nova*, composers such as Machaut were using sophisticated rhythmic notation in both sacred and secular polyphony.


## slide-5 — Renaissance

### label
Renaissance

### kicker
c. 1400–1600

### title
Renaissance

### tracks
Thomas Tallis | Spem in alium | 0:16 - 1:28 | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z3FJxDsa-5k | 16 | 88

### note
40 independent voices

### speaker-notes
- **The Renaissance was an international network:** Composers moved among courts and chapels across Europe, carrying techniques between regions while local languages and traditions remained distinct. Music printing, especially after Petrucci’s influential 1501 collection, accelerated that circulation and allowed styles to travel farther than manuscript copying alone.
- **The human voice remained the central model:** Masses and motets weave several independent lines of nearly equal importance into imitative polyphony, with a melodic idea passed from voice to voice. Increasing use of thirds and sixths also created the smoother, fuller sonority now strongly associated with the period.
- **Humanism made words more musically consequential:** Composers pursued clearer text setting, expressive rhetoric, and word painting, allowing musical motion to suggest rising, falling, running, or weeping. Secular madrigals and chansons expanded this approach to humor, desire, nature, grief, and dramatic characterization.
- **Religious upheaval changed participation and style:** The Reformation encouraged vernacular congregational traditions such as the Lutheran chorale, while the Catholic Counter-Reformation renewed debate over whether complex polyphony obscured sacred words. Tallis and Byrd show how composers adapted across dangerous shifts in English religion and royal power.
- **Music increasingly occupied domestic and spatial worlds:** Matched consorts of viols or recorders brought ensemble music into courts and homes, while works such as Tallis’s forty-part *Spem in alium* made physical space part of the composition. Listen for sound traveling among eight choirs, expanding into massive unity, and thinning again.

## slide-6 — Baroque

### label
Baroque

### kicker

J. S. Bach | Chaconne · Partita No. 2 | 0:19 - 1:48 | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ngjEVKxQCWs | 19 | 108

### facts
**Barroco** (Portuguese) - "misshapen pearl"
Doctrine of the Affections
Motor Rhythm

### speaker-notes
- **Around 1600, composers sought more direct dramatic expression:** A solo voice supported by chords made words easier to understand than dense Renaissance polyphony, helping establish monody, recitative, and opera. Monteverdi’s *Orfeo* shows how voice and instrumental color could define characters, while Venice’s public opera houses later turned court spectacle into ticketed entertainment.
- **Basso continuo and tonality power the music forward:** A bass instrument supports the line while a chordal player improvises harmonies from shorthand figures. At the same time, increasingly stable major-minor keys and functional bass motion create a strong sense of departure, tension, sequence, and return.
- **Contrast and motor rhythm become structural forces:** Concertos oppose soloist and ensemble, colors answer one another, and recurring ritornellos alternate with changing episodes. Beneath that contrast, a persistent pulse or rhythmic cell gives passages of sequence, ornament, and virtuosity their kinetic drive.
- **A movement often explores one governing emotional world:** The Baroque theory of the affections is a useful listening frame because a movement may intensify a dominant state rather than pass through a cinematic series of moods. Ornamentation, articulation, timing, cadenzas, and continuo realization made performers active collaborators in shaping that expression.
- **New instruments grew within old systems of employment:** The violin family rose to prominence, woodwinds specialized, and orchestras stabilized around strings and continuo. Yet composers still worked under practical institutional demands—Bach for churches and courts, Vivaldi for the Ospedale, and Handel for theaters and royal occasions.

### era-notes
Clarity · balance · proportion
Melody-led · symmetrical phrases
Contrast · development · return

### speaker-notes
- **Classical style reflects Enlightenment ideals of clarity and argument:** Clearly shaped melodies, lighter accompaniments, and balanced question-and-answer phrases help listeners remember, compare, and recognize ideas. Haydn’s humor works because he can extend, interrupt, or misdirect expectations that the audience already understands.
- **Sonata form turns contrast into a dramatic journey:** An exposition establishes themes and key areas, a development destabilizes and recombines them, and a recapitulation restores the home key. Its power lies less in memorizing a diagram than in hearing departure, conflict, transformation, and return.
- **New genres and instruments become laboratories for conversation:** The symphony organizes large-scale orchestral argument, the string quartet gives four players unusual equality, and the concerto stages cooperation and competition between soloist and ensemble. The fortepiano adds touch-sensitive dynamics that the harpsichord cannot provide.
- **The orchestra and its audience become more standardized:** A string core with paired winds and horns gives composers a dependable palette for contrast, while expanding public concert life brings music to paying urban audiences outside courts and churches. Reputation, publication, repeat performance, and audience appeal increasingly matter alongside patronage.
- **Careers reveal the transition into a modern musical economy:** Haydn’s Esterházy employment gave him a stable ensemble for experimentation, while Mozart pursued commissions, teaching, publication, and subscription concerts in Vienna. Beethoven then stretched inherited forms toward Romantic scale, making Classical structure feel longer, more forceful, and psychologically charged.

### era-notes
Emotion · individuality · imagination
Expanding sound · richer color
Rubato · chromaticism · virtuosity

### speaker-notes
- **Romanticism grows from social upheaval and a new image of the artist:** Revolution, industrialization, nationalism, and the expanding middle class changed musical institutions and audiences. Composers increasingly treated music as confession, dream, memory, landscape, or personal struggle, recasting the artist as an independent visionary even when publishers and patrons still paid the bills.
- **The era moves between public spectacle and private intimacy:** Large concert halls and opera houses encourage enormous orchestras and theatrical grandeur, while the middle-class parlor supports songs, character pieces, chamber music, and home lessons. Romantic expression therefore ranges from mass experience to the feeling of overhearing a private thought.
- **Harmony and time become vehicles for longing:** Chromaticism, delayed cadences, distant keys, and expressive dissonance make tonal arrival less certain, while rubato lets performers stretch and compress a phrase around an underlying pulse. In Chopin especially, melody can seem to breathe independently above the accompaniment.
- **Technology expands color, volume, and celebrity:** Stronger pianos, brass valves, larger orchestras, new percussion, and more powerful string setups answer the demands of bigger rooms. Virtuosos such as Paganini and Liszt turn difficult technique and stage presence into international celebrity, while composers such as Berlioz and Wagner make orchestration central to a work’s identity.
- **Music increasingly carries stories and identities:** Nationalist works draw on dances, legends, landscapes, and folk materials; program music connects sound to literature or nature; art song turns poetry into psychological drama; and operatic leitmotifs create recurring musical memory. That last technique becomes a direct ancestor of thematic storytelling in film scores.

### era-notes
Tonal boundaries loosen
Rhythm · Timbre
Dissonance as structure
Many languages

### speaker-notes
- **Modernism is a field of competing answers, not one style:** As late-Romantic harmony reached an extreme, some composers dissolved its edges, some rejected tonality, some looked backward, and others built new languages from rhythm, timbre, folk traditions, or popular music. The period makes more sense as a debate over musical possibilities than as one unified sound.
- **Color and rhythm can replace traditional harmonic direction:** Debussy uses modes, whole-tone collections, parallel chords, and unresolved sonorities to make harmony behave like atmosphere; Ravel combines similar color with exact construction. Stravinsky’s shifting accents and layered patterns show that rhythm can generate form as powerfully as melody or key.
- **Composers rebuild musical order in radically different ways:** Schoenberg’s atonality and twelve-tone method remove the expectation of a home key, while neoclassicism reuses older forms and ensembles with modern harmony and rhythm. Both approaches reject Romantic excess, but one invents a new system as the other transforms historical models.
- **Jazz, vernacular music, and folk research reshape the concert tradition:** Gershwin brings jazz language to the concert stage, Copland seeks an accessible American sound, and Bartók uses recordings of rural musicians to absorb unfamiliar rhythmic and melodic practices. These exchanges also require attention to race, attribution, and who received institutional recognition.
- **Politics and technology change where music lives:** Fascist and Soviet regimes censored, displaced, promoted, or condemned composers, while two world wars forced artists and traditions across continents. Recording, radio, and cinema let performances circulate without musicians present and made the studio, microphone, and moving image part of musical creation.

### era-notes
Pluralism · Minimalism
Orchestral sound in film
Electronics · New Media

### speaker-notes
- **After 1950 there is no single mainstream:** Serialism, minimalism, experimentalism, electronic music, neoromantic writing, film music, multimedia, and cross-genre work coexist. “Contemporary” therefore names a period more reliably than a shared sound, and the most useful first question is often what system organizes a particular piece.
- **Composers redefine how much control a score should exert:** Postwar serialists extend ordered systems into rhythm, dynamics, and articulation, while Cage and other experimentalists invite chance and performer choice. A work might prescribe every parameter or define only a process, duration, and field of possibilities.
- **Electronic tools make sound and the studio compositional materials:** Tape, synthesizers, sampling, computers, layering, editing, spatial placement, and effects create sounds no acoustic instrument can produce. The finished work may be a recording, installation, or soundtrack rather than a score intended to document the same live performance repeatedly.
- **Minimalism changes the listener’s sense of time:** Repetition, steady pulse, gradual process, and small shifts move attention toward phase, accent, and accumulating layers. What initially seems static can become highly active once the ear adjusts to transformation on a slower timescale.
- **Contemporary music crosses media, genres, and traditions:** Orchestras combine with electronics and production techniques; concert music exchanges ideas with jazz, rock, hip-hop, ambient, folk, and dance music; and scores for film, games, and installations may loop or branch. Global collaboration expands the palette, but it also makes cultural context, attribution, and power essential parts of the conversation.
