## slide-4 — Early & Medieval

### label
Early & Medieval

### kicker
c. 500–1400

### title
Early &
Medieval

### tracks
Pérotin | Viderunt omnes | 0:12 - 0:42 | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q2JvIyStzNA | 12 | 42

### note
Monophony · Polyphony · Church modes not scales

### speaker-notes
- **Start with the scale of the era:** “Medieval music” covers roughly nine centuries, so it is not one fixed sound. The music of an early monastery, a twelfth-century cathedral, and a fourteenth-century court could differ as much as several later musical eras do from one another.
- **Most surviving sources come from institutions that could write things down:** Monasteries, cathedrals, and royal courts had trained scribes, libraries, and money for manuscripts. That survival bias is why sacred music dominates the written record even though people also sang work songs, dance music, love songs, and stories.
- **Plainchant begins with one communal melodic line:** The flexible rhythm follows the words rather than a regular beat, and the unified voices make the text feel collective rather than individual. The sound is restrained, but it was never meant to be emotionally empty.
- **Notation changed what music could become:** Early chant was transmitted largely by memory, with marks called neumes serving as reminders. More precise staff notation, associated especially with Guido of Arezzo in the eleventh century, made it easier to teach melodies, preserve them, and eventually coordinate several voices.
- **Modes organize the music differently from modern major and minor keys:** A mode gives the melody a characteristic home range and pattern of intervals, but listeners should not expect the strong dominant-to-tonic pull that later tonal harmony creates. The result can feel suspended, circular, or open-ended to modern ears.
- **Polyphony begins as an addition to chant:** In early organum, a second voice moves alongside a familiar sacred melody. Over time the added voices become more independent, turning a single line into an architectural structure of sustained notes, moving upper parts, and carefully controlled dissonance.
- **Notre-Dame Paris became a center of musical experiment:** In the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, composers associated with the cathedral—especially Léonin and Pérotin—worked on an unprecedented scale. Pérotin’s music can place several rhythmically active voices above a very slow-moving chant foundation.
- **Rhythmic notation was a major breakthrough:** Notre-Dame composers used recurring rhythmic patterns that helped singers coordinate long polyphonic passages. Once rhythm could be represented with greater precision, composers could design larger and more intricate structures.
- **The building participates in the performance:** Long reverberation blends tones, magnifies sustained notes, and turns silence into part of the phrase. Ask the audience to imagine the stone cathedral as an acoustic technology, not merely a backdrop.
- **Secular song existed beside church music:** Troubadours, trouvères, and related poet-musicians sang about courtly love, politics, devotion, and satire. Much of this repertory is monophonic, but it reminds us that medieval musical life extended far beyond worship.
- **Women were important creators despite limited access to institutions:** Hildegard of Bingen, a twelfth-century abbess, wrote sacred songs with unusually wide ranges and vivid imagery. Her surviving work shows that authority, theology, poetry, and composition could meet in one voice.
- **The late Middle Ages brought greater rhythmic freedom:** Fourteenth-century *Ars nova* notation allowed more complex divisions of rhythm and supported composers such as Guillaume de Machaut. By this point, sacred and secular polyphony could be highly sophisticated, anticipating several concerns of the Renaissance.


## slide-5 — Renaissance

### label
Renaissance

### kicker
c. 1400–1600

### title
Renaissance

### tracks
Thomas Tallis | Spem in alium | 0:16 - 1:28 | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z3FJxDsa-5k | 16 | 88

### note
40 independent voices

### speaker-notes
- **Think of the Renaissance as a network, not only an Italian rebirth:** Composers moved among courts and chapels across present-day France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Italy, Germany, Spain, and England. Traveling musicians helped create an international style while local languages and traditions remained distinct.
- **The human voice remained the central instrument:** Masses and motets were constructed from several independent melodic lines, often equal in importance. The musical ideal was not a soloist with accompaniment but a community of voices whose meanings emerge through their relationships.
- **Imitative polyphony creates audible continuity:** A melodic idea enters in one voice and is answered by another, then another. Listeners can follow the idea moving through the group even when the overall texture is dense.
- **Harmony begins to sound fuller and more familiar:** Composers increasingly favored thirds and sixths, intervals that contribute to the smoother sonority associated with Renaissance music. Modal thinking continued, but the vertical blend of voices became increasingly important.
- **Composers became more attentive to the meaning of words:** Humanist interest in rhetoric and poetry encouraged clearer text setting, expressive melodic gestures, and later “word painting,” in which the musical shape illustrates an image such as rising, falling, running, or weeping.
- **Music printing accelerated circulation:** In 1501 Ottaviano Petrucci issued an influential printed collection of polyphonic music in Venice. Music still remained expensive, but printing allowed works and styles to travel more widely than hand-copying alone.
- **Patronage broadened during the sixteenth century:** Churches and courts remained essential, but wealthy amateurs and the growing print market also supported composers. Music became increasingly available for skilled domestic performers as well as professional chapel musicians.
- **The Reformation changed who sang and in what language:** Lutheran chorales invited congregational participation in German, while other Protestant traditions developed their own approaches to worship. Catholic Counter-Reformation composers also reconsidered how complex music could remain reverent and keep sacred words intelligible.
- **Secular genres flourished:** Italian madrigals, French chansons, German songs, English consort songs, and Spanish villancicos allowed composers to explore humor, desire, landscape, grief, and dramatic characterization outside the Mass.
- **Instrumental families were built in matched sizes:** Consorts of viols, recorders, and other instruments could reproduce the blended ranges of a vocal ensemble. Lutes and keyboard instruments also became central to courtly and domestic music-making.
- **Tallis turns space into musical form:** *Spem in alium* uses forty independent voices arranged as eight choirs of five. Rather than hearing forty lines at once, listen for sound passing from group to group, gathering into massive tutti moments, and then thinning again.
- **English sacred music reflects dangerous political change:** Tallis and William Byrd worked across shifts between Catholic and Protestant monarchs. Their careers show how a composer could adapt language, style, and liturgical purpose while navigating questions of faith and royal authority.

### speaker-notes
- **The name came later and was not originally a compliment:** “Baroque” is often connected with the Portuguese *barroco*, an irregular or misshapen pearl. Later historians applied it to art and music that seemed ornate, dramatic, or extravagant compared with Classical restraint.
- **Around 1600, composers sought a more direct kind of expression:** A solo voice supported by chords could make words easier to understand than dense Renaissance polyphony. This new emphasis on melody and accompaniment helped establish monody, recitative, and opera.
- **Opera united the arts:** Music, poetry, acting, scenery, costume, and stage machinery worked together to produce emotional spectacle. Monteverdi’s *Orfeo* of 1607 is an early landmark because it uses vocal style and instrumental color to define characters and dramatic situations.
- **Opera moved from court celebration to public entertainment:** Venice opened its first public opera house in 1637. Audiences who bought tickets—not only invited aristocrats—could now shape repertory, star culture, and the economics of musical theater.
- **Basso continuo is the era’s harmonic engine:** A bass instrument reinforces the line while a harpsichord, organ, lute, theorbo, or harp realizes the chords, often from numerical shorthand. The player was expected to improvise the exact voicing rather than reproduce every note from a fully written part.
- **The tonal system becomes increasingly goal-directed:** Major and minor keys, functional bass motion, and strong cadences gradually replace the looser modal organization of earlier periods. This gives Baroque music its powerful sense of departure, tension, sequence, and return.
- **Contrast becomes a formal principle:** Concertos oppose soloists and ensemble; dynamics can alternate in blocks; and instrumental colors answer one another. Vivaldi’s ritornello designs repeatedly bring the full group back between changing solo episodes.
- **Motor rhythm creates momentum:** A repeated pulse or short rhythmic cell can persist through an entire movement. The steadiness below allows sequences, ornament, harmonic tension, and virtuoso passagework above it to feel almost kinetic.
- **The “affections” offer a useful listening frame:** A movement often sustains and explores a dominant emotional state rather than moving through a modern cinematic sequence of moods. The performer’s ornamentation, articulation, and timing intensify that emotional world.
- **Improvisation was part of normal performance practice:** Continuo realization, cadenzas, embellishments, repeated-section ornaments, and preluding were expected skills. A Baroque score often supplies a framework for performance rather than every detail of a fixed, definitive version.
- **Instrument building reshaped the ensemble:** The violin family gained prominence, celebrated makers refined string instruments, woodwinds became more specialized, and the orchestra began to stabilize around a string core with continuo.
- **Patronage still determined daily musical life:** Bach served churches and courts, Vivaldi taught and composed for the Ospedale della Pietà, and Handel built a career around theaters and royal occasions. “Composer” was usually a job embedded in an institution, with constant deadlines and practical performers in mind.

### era-notes
Clarity · balance · proportion

### speaker-notes
- **The date boundaries are approximate:** Elements of the Classical style appear before 1750, and Romantic tendencies emerge well before 1820. The era is best understood as a changing set of priorities rather than a switch flipped at Bach’s death.
- **The Enlightenment favored clarity, proportion, and intelligible argument:** Musical ideas are often presented in sharply defined phrases so a listener can remember, compare, and recognize their return. The ideal resembles persuasive conversation rather than uninterrupted decorative complexity.
- **Melody moves to the foreground:** Instead of several equally active contrapuntal lines, a clear tune is often supported by a simpler accompaniment. Alberti bass patterns are one familiar way keyboard music sustains harmony without competing with the melody.
- **Balanced phrases establish expectations that composers can play with:** Four- and eight-measure units often feel like questions and answers. Haydn’s humor frequently comes from extending, interrupting, or misdirecting those expectations.
- **Sonata form turns contrast into drama:** An opening section presents contrasting themes and keys; a development destabilizes and recombines them; a recapitulation returns them to the home key. The power comes less from following a rigid diagram than from hearing departure, conflict, and resolution.
- **The symphony and string quartet become major public and private laboratories:** The symphony lets composers organize large-scale orchestral argument, while the quartet invites four individual instruments to converse with unusual equality and detail.
- **The orchestra becomes more standardized:** Strings remain the center, with paired winds and horns increasingly common; clarinets join more regularly later in the century. Composers can now build drama by changing color as well as harmony and volume.
- **The fortepiano gradually displaces the harpsichord:** Its hammer action allows players to shape loudness through touch, giving composers new possibilities for accent, dynamic surprise, and lyrical shading.
- **Patronage and the marketplace overlap:** Haydn spent decades in Esterházy service, where steady employment gave him an orchestra for experimentation. Mozart tried to build a more independent life in Vienna through commissions, teaching, publication, benefit concerts, and subscriptions.
- **Public concert life expands with the urban middle class:** Music increasingly reaches paying audiences outside court and church. The composer must think not only about a patron’s needs but also about reputation, publication, repeat performances, and audience appeal.
- **The concerto stages a social drama:** The soloist alternates between cooperation and competition with the orchestra. In Mozart’s concertos, the piano can seem to enter a conversation already underway, then reshape it through intimacy, wit, or virtuosity.
- **Beethoven stretches Classical language toward Romantic scale:** In works such as the *Eroica*, familiar forms become longer, more forceful, and psychologically charged. His music is effective here precisely because its disruptions are measured against a Classical structure the audience can still hear underneath.

### era-notes
Emotion · individuality · imagination
Expanding sound · richer color

### speaker-notes
- **Romanticism grows out of political and social upheaval:** Revolutions, nationalism, industrialization, and the expansion of the middle class changed who made music, who paid for it, and what audiences expected art to express.
- **The individual imagination becomes a central subject:** Composers increasingly present music as confession, dream, memory, landscape, supernatural encounter, or personal struggle. The artist is recast from skilled servant toward independent visionary—even when the practical reality still depends on publishers, patrons, and audiences.
- **The era lives at two opposite scales:** Grand opera houses and concert halls encourage enormous orchestras and public spectacle, while the middle-class parlor supports intimate songs, character pieces, chamber music, and music lessons at home.
- **Harmony becomes more chromatic and less settled:** Composers intensify longing by delaying cadences, moving through distant keys, and treating dissonance as expressive color. A home key may remain important, but the path back to it becomes increasingly adventurous.
- **Rubato makes musical time feel personal:** A performer can stretch and compress the phrase for expression while maintaining an underlying sense of pulse. This is especially important in Chopin, where the melody can seem to breathe above the accompaniment.
- **The orchestra grows in size and color:** Larger string sections, expanded woodwinds and brass, new percussion, and improved instrument mechanisms make extreme dynamics and novel combinations possible. Berlioz and Wagner treat orchestration as part of the composition’s identity, not merely its clothing.
- **Instrument technology responds to larger rooms and virtuoso demands:** The piano gains a stronger iron frame, wider range, and greater sustaining power; brass valves improve chromatic flexibility; string setups evolve to project more forcefully.
- **Virtuosos become international celebrities:** Paganini and Liszt built public personas around seemingly impossible technique and dramatic stage presence. Their fame helped elevate performers from employees of courts to recognizable cultural stars.
- **Nationalism becomes a musical resource and a political claim:** Composers draw on folk melodies, dances, legends, landscapes, and languages to imagine national identity. The same practice can express cultural independence, nostalgia, exoticism, or the politics of empire, depending on who is representing whom.
- **Program music tells or suggests a story without words:** The tone poem, program symphony, and descriptive miniature invite listeners to connect sound with literature, nature, biography, or visual scenes. Other composers defend “absolute music,” creating a major nineteenth-century debate about whether music needs an external subject.
- **The art song turns poetry into psychological drama:** In a Lied, the piano does more than accompany; it can represent weather, footsteps, memory, or an unspoken emotional truth beneath the singer’s words.
- **Opera develops recurring musical memory:** Leitmotifs can identify a person, object, idea, or fate, then change as the drama changes. This technique becomes one of the clearest bridges from nineteenth-century opera to twentieth-century film scoring.
- **Use the excerpts as contrasting Romantic personalities:** Beethoven and Chopin bring urgency through different kinds of motion; Tchaikovsky projects public grandeur; Brahms and Schubert turn inward; Rachmaninoff stretches longing across long lines; Saint-Saëns lets virtuosity drive the drama.

### era-notes

### speaker-notes
- **Modernism is a field of competing answers, not one style:** Around 1900 many composers felt that late-Romantic harmony had reached an extreme. Some dissolved its edges, some rejected it, some looked backward, and others built new languages from rhythm, folk traditions, or popular music.
- **Debussy weakens traditional harmonic gravity:** Whole-tone and pentatonic collections, modal colors, unresolved sonorities, and parallel chord movement allow harmony to function as atmosphere rather than a chain of problems demanding resolution.
- **Ravel shows that innovation can coexist with precision:** His music is often sensuous in color but exact in construction. Orchestration can make a familiar harmony feel new by changing register, articulation, spacing, and instrumental blend.
- **Rhythm becomes an independent structural force:** Stravinsky’s shifting accents, layered patterns, and irregular groupings demonstrate that rhythm can generate form as powerfully as melody and harmony. The 1913 premiere of *The Rite of Spring* became a symbol—sometimes mythologized—of modern artistic disruption.
- **Atonality removes the expectation of a home key:** Schoenberg and his circle explored music in which all twelve chromatic pitches could participate without conventional tonal hierarchy. His later twelve-tone method organized those pitches through an ordered series rather than traditional key relationships.
- **Modern composers also revive the past:** Neoclassicism reuses older forms, gestures, and ensembles with modern harmony and rhythm. Looking backward could be a way to escape Romantic excess rather than a return to historical innocence.
- **Jazz transforms the concert world:** Syncopation, blue-note inflection, improvisational energy, and new instrumental colors enter works by Gershwin, Ravel, Milhaud, Stravinsky, and others. This exchange also raises important questions about race, attribution, and who received institutional recognition.
- **American composers debate what an American concert sound should be:** Gershwin brings jazz language onto the concert stage, while Copland eventually seeks a more open and accessible style shaped by vernacular music, dance, landscape, and a desire to reach Depression-era audiences.
- **Folk traditions become both research and raw material:** Composers such as Bartók used early recording technology to document rural musicians, then absorbed rhythmic and melodic practices into a modern language rather than simply decorating European forms with a borrowed tune.
- **Politics could make musical style dangerous:** Fascist and Soviet governments censored, displaced, promoted, or condemned composers. Shostakovich’s career is often heard through the tension between public triumph, official pressure, irony, and private meaning—though no single code explains every passage.
- **Technology changes listening as well as composing:** Recordings, radio, and cinema let music travel without the composer or performer present. Audiences could hear the same interpretation repeatedly, and composers increasingly wrote for microphones, recording studios, and moving images.
- **Timbre becomes part of the architecture:** New percussion, unusual ensembles, extended techniques, and extreme registers allow composers to organize a piece through changing sound color. Invite the audience to listen for texture before deciding whether the harmony is “beautiful.”
- **The two World Wars fracture musical geography:** Exile and migration relocate composers and traditions across Europe and the Americas. Modern music is therefore a history of institutions and forced movement as much as a story of abstract stylistic progress.

### speaker-notes
- **After 1950 there is no single mainstream:** Serialism, minimalism, experimentalism, electronic music, spectralism, neoromantic writing, film music, multimedia work, and cross-genre composition coexist. “Contemporary” names a time period more reliably than a shared sound.
- **Postwar serialists sought new systems of order:** Some composers extended serial thinking beyond pitch to rhythm, dynamics, and articulation. Festivals and studios in places such as Darmstadt became centers for rebuilding European musical culture through radical experimentation.
- **Other composers welcomed chance and performer choice:** John Cage and many experimentalists questioned the idea that the composer must control every sound. A score might define a process, a duration, or a set of possibilities rather than one fixed sequence of notes.
- **Electronic music makes sound itself a compositional material:** Tape, oscillators, synthesizers, sampling, computers, and digital audio allow composers to build sounds that no acoustic instrument can produce and to transform recorded sound beyond recognition.
- **The recording studio becomes an instrument:** Layering, editing, spatial placement, and effects can be part of the work’s original conception rather than documentation added after a performance. The “definitive object” may be a recording, installation, or film soundtrack instead of a printed score.
- **Minimalism rethinks musical change:** Repetition, steady pulse, gradual process, and small shifts ask the listener to notice transformation at a different timescale. What initially seems static can become intensely active when attention moves to phase, accent, and accumulating layers.
- **Global exchange expands the palette, but context matters:** Composers increasingly engage musical systems, instruments, and collaborators from outside the European concert tradition. The richest work treats those traditions as living knowledge rather than anonymous color available for extraction.
- **Genre boundaries become porous:** Concert composers draw from jazz, rock, ambient music, hip-hop, folk practices, and electronic dance music, while artists in those fields adopt orchestral and experimental techniques. Institutions may still label the results, but the music often resists the border.
- **Film scoring carries nineteenth-century storytelling into mass culture:** Leitmotifs, harmonic memory, orchestral color, and synchronized dramatic pacing descend in part from opera and silent-film accompaniment. The cinema becomes one of the largest modern audiences for symphonic sound.
- **John Williams helped revive the symphonic film score:** *Star Wars* deliberately used an emotionally familiar late-Romantic orchestral language and recurring themes at a time when electronic and popular styles dominated much science-fiction cinema. Its success helped return large-scale orchestral scoring to the center of Hollywood practice.
- **Contemporary orchestration is hybrid by default:** Acoustic orchestra, processed sound, synthesizers, found sound, percussion from multiple traditions, and close-miked solo instruments can occupy the same score. The composer may think simultaneously like an orchestrator, producer, and sound designer.
- **New media change the shape of a piece:** Music can respond to games, installations, virtual spaces, interactive systems, or nonlinear storytelling. Unlike a concert work with a fixed duration, an interactive score may need to loop, branch, or transform without losing its identity.
- **Access and institutions are changing:** Digital distribution lets independent composers release work without a major publisher, while conservatories, orchestras, film studios, and streaming platforms still shape whose music receives resources and repeat exposure.
- **A useful listening question is no longer “What style is this?”:** Ask instead what system organizes the sound—pulse, process, narrative, texture, technology, quotation, or collaboration. Contemporary music often becomes clearer when the listener identifies its rules before judging its surface.
