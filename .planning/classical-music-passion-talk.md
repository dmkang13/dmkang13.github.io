# Classical Music Passion Talk

Private planning document. This file is intentionally stored in `.planning/` and must not be linked or displayed on the website.

## Core intent

The presentation should let the audience discover these ideas through listening, stories, contrast, and participation. Do not state them as an agenda or write them out on the slides.

- Classical music can spark imagination and personal storytelling.
- The category contains far more variety than its stereotype suggests.
- Music carries history, public meaning, and private emotion, even without words.

## Narrative method

- Begin with Dale as a musician, using a few personal JPGs rather than performance video.
- Define classical music briefly, then move chronologically.
- Give every musical era one program slide containing every work used from that era.
- Give every listening break or activity its own full slide and treat it as a distinct topic.
- Let historical context change how the audience hears each excerpt.
- Avoid explaining the emotional meaning as settled fact. Invite listeners to form an image first, then reveal the historical or personal context.
- End in the present, where film music makes the same storytelling process easy to recognize.

## Musical eras and listening breaks

### Slide 4: Early and Medieval, c. 500-1400

**Works on this slide**

- Pérotin, *Viderunt omnes*

**Historical and storytelling notes**

- Correction: *Viderunt omnes* is associated with Pérotin, not Hildegard von Bingen.
- Pérotin's four-voice setting was written for Christmas worship at Notre-Dame in Paris around 1198.
- Use the cathedral as the story: the sustained lower chant and moving upper voices can feel like sound turned into architecture.
- Listening prompt: "What kind of space does this sound create?"

**Optional choice**

- If Hildegard is personally important, add one of her own works as a second example rather than attaching her name to *Viderunt omnes*.

### Slide 5: Renaissance, c. 1400-1600

**Works on this slide**

- Thomas Tallis, *Spem in alium*

**Historical and storytelling notes**

- Tallis wrote the motet for 40 independent vocal parts.
- The parts surround the listener in groups, making the music feel spatial long before electronic surround sound.
- The emotional story can emerge from scale: one voice begins alone, a community gradually forms, and the full ensemble becomes overwhelming.
- Listening prompt: "Can you hear the sound travel through the room?"

### Slide 6: Baroque, c. 1600-1750

**Works on this slide**

- Claudio Monteverdi, "Dal mio Permesso amato" from *L'Orfeo*
- Johann Pachelbel, *Canon in D*
- Johann Sebastian Bach, Chaconne from Partita No. 2 in D minor

**Historical and storytelling notes**

- Monteverdi's *L'Orfeo* shows how music, theater, and myth joined at the beginning of opera. The character of Music introduces herself and promises to move the human soul.
- Pachelbel's Canon did not begin as a wedding standard. A twentieth-century recording revival helped turn it into one centuries later.
- The repeating bass under Pachelbel's changing lines provides a useful metaphor: the ground stays fixed while the story grows above it.
- Bach's Chaconne builds a long emotional journey from repeating harmonic foundations without supplying a title or plot.
- Avoid repeating the popular claim that Bach wrote the Chaconne as a memorial for his wife. Scholars have not established that story.
- The Bach excerpt can appear here in the era program and return later for the audience activity.

### Slide 7: Listening break, Vivaldi's *The Four Seasons*

**Topic**

- Why *The Four Seasons* rewards active listening.

**Flow**

1. Show a short translated line from one of the accompanying sonnets.
2. Ask the audience to predict what musical gesture might represent it.
3. Play the excerpt.
4. Reveal the matching image, such as birds, wind, rain, a storm, or chattering teeth.

**Historical and storytelling notes**

- The concertos were published with sonnets that describe scenes in each season, possibly written by Vivaldi himself.
- This is a clear early example of instrumental music carrying a recognizable narrative.
- Keep the interaction focused on one season rather than sampling all four.

### Slide 8: Classical era, c. 1750-1820

**Works on this slide**

- Joseph Haydn, Cello Concerto No. 1 in C major, Mvt. 3
- Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, Piano Concerto No. 23 in A major, Mvt. 2
- Ludwig van Beethoven, Symphony No. 9, Mvt. 4, "Ode to Joy"

**Historical and storytelling notes**

- Haydn's concerto disappeared for almost two centuries before a copy resurfaced in Prague in 1961. Its survival creates a literal rediscovery story.
- The finale lets the cello behave like a quick, witty protagonist trading ideas with the orchestra.
- Mozart's second movement moves into F-sharp minor, an unusually dark key inside an A-major concerto.
- Mozart was working on *The Marriage of Figaro* at roughly the same time. The orchestra and soloist can be heard as characters exchanging lines.
- Listening contrast: Haydn's outward energy beside Mozart's inward, almost operatic voice.
- Beethoven appears here as the Classical tradition expanding beyond its old boundaries. The Ninth's finale sets Schiller's poem and adds solo voices and chorus to the symphony.
- Beethoven could not hear the audience's applause at the 1824 premiere because of his deafness.

### Slide 9: Listening break, hearing character without a plot

**Part 1: Watch**

- Show the selected scene from *Amadeus* with only a short subtitle: "Salieri hears Mozart's music."
- No contextual notes or historical explanation on the slide.

**Part 2: Bach Chaconne activity**

- Return to the Bach work introduced on the Baroque slide.
- Play the excerpt before offering historical interpretation.
- Ask the audience to write or say three things: a place, a character, and an emotional change.
- Compare descriptions without naming a correct answer.

**Purpose**

- Demonstrate that listeners already know how to create a story from abstract music.

### Slide 10: Romantic era, c. 1820-1900

**Works on this slide**

- Ludwig van Beethoven, Piano Sonata No. 14, Mvt. 3, commonly called the "Moonlight" Sonata
- Frédéric Chopin, Étude Op. 10 No. 4
- Frédéric Chopin, Étude Op. 10 No. 9
- Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky, Piano Concerto No. 1, opening
- Johannes Brahms, Piano Trio, exact work and movement to be confirmed
- Franz Liszt, *Liebestraum* No. 3
- Sergei Rachmaninoff, Piano Concerto No. 2, Mvt. 2, Adagio sostenuto
- Edvard Grieg, Piano Concerto in A minor, movement to be confirmed

**Historical and storytelling notes**

- The era slide should feel abundant, with every work visible in one flowing typographic composition rather than separate cards.
- Beethoven appears again as the bridge into Romantic subjectivity. He called Op. 27 No. 2 *Sonata quasi una fantasia*; the "Moonlight" nickname came later from critic Ludwig Rellstab's image of moonlight over Lake Lucerne.
- The nickname itself shows a listener's imagined story permanently changing how later audiences hear a work.
- Tchaikovsky's First Piano Concerto survived harsh initial criticism from Nikolai Rubinstein before becoming one of the repertory's defining concertos.
- If the Brahms selection is Piano Trio No. 1 in B major, Op. 8, note that Brahms returned to it about 35 years later and extensively revised his younger self.
- *Liebestraum* No. 3 grew from Liszt's song setting of a poem urging people to love while they still can, before they stand grieving at a grave.
- Rachmaninoff composed his Second Piano Concerto after treatment for depression and creative paralysis, then dedicated it to physician Nikolai Dahl.
- The slide's emotional progression can move from virtuoso urgency to tenderness, memory, and recovery.

### Slide 11: Listening break, a story for Chopin

**Work**

- Chopin, Étude Op. 10 No. 9

**Activity**

1. Do not show an image before listening.
2. Ask the audience to notice who or what seems to be moving.
3. Play the selected excerpt.
4. Collect a few short descriptions.
5. Show contrasting images only afterward, demonstrating that the same music can support several stories.

**Purpose**

- Give the audience ownership of the imaginative process.

### Slide 12: Modern era, approximately 1880-1950

The overlap in dates is intentional. Musical eras do not change on January 1 of a new century.

**Works on this slide**

- Claude Debussy, *Deux Arabesques*, number to be confirmed
- Maurice Ravel, *Pavane pour une infante défunte*, orchestral version
- Samuel Barber, Violin Concerto, Op. 14, movement to be confirmed
- Samuel Barber, *Adagio for Strings*
- Jean Sibelius, Violin Concerto in D minor, Mvt. 3
- Dmitri Shostakovich, Symphony No. 5, Mvt. 4
- George Gershwin, *Rhapsody in Blue*
- Aaron Copland, "Corral Nocturne" from *Rodeo*

**Corrections and placement**

- Correct spelling: *Deux Arabesques*.
- Ravel composed the piano version of the *Pavane* in 1899 and orchestrated it later, so it belongs at the Romantic-to-Modern threshold.
- Barber wrote one Violin Concerto, Op. 14. Do not label it "No. 1."
- Sibelius wrote one Violin Concerto. Do not label it "No. 1."
- Use Shostakovich's Symphony No. 5, Mvt. 4 for the story about public rehabilitation and the disputed meaning of its triumphant ending.
- Symphony No. 4 supplies a different story: Shostakovich withdrew it before its premiere, and the public did not hear it until 1961. It has only three movements, so "Symphony No. 4, Mvt. 4" is not possible.
- *Rhapsody in Blue* premiered in 1924 and belongs in this era. Ferde Grofé created the familiar full-symphony arrangement in 1942.
- Correct spelling: Copland's "Corral Nocturne." It comes from Agnes de Mille's 1942 ballet *Rodeo*.

**Historical and storytelling notes**

- Ravel warned listeners not to take the *Pavane* title as the story of a real dead princess. The evocative title still encourages listeners to invent one.
- Barber adapted the *Adagio* from his String Quartet. Later performances during national mourning gave it a public meaning beyond its original context.
- Sibelius pursued the violin seriously but did not become the virtuoso he imagined. The concerto lets the soloist inhabit that unrealized dream.
- Shostakovich's Fifth followed official denunciation. Its finale can sound victorious, forced, or both, making hidden emotion part of the listening experience.
- Gershwin and Grofé brought jazz sonorities into the concert hall while crossing boundaries between popular and concert traditions.
- Copland and de Mille created a specifically American stage story rather than imitating a European past.

### Slide 13: Listening break, the orchestra as a human voice

**Work**

- Barber, *Adagio for Strings*

**Activity**

1. Ask the audience to imagine a single person trying to speak through the string section.
2. Listen for breath, a phrase that strains upward, a breaking point, silence, and the attempt to continue.
3. Ask where the voice seems to change emotionally.
4. Reveal the work's later association with public mourning only after the first responses.

**Examples to reveal briefly**

- Rising line: reaching or pleading.
- Repeated note: insistence, stammer, or sob.
- Long held tone: one sustained breath.
- Crescendo: gathering pressure.
- Rest: breath, interruption, or a voice breaking.
- Falling line: sigh or release.
- Dissonance resolving: tension becoming relief.

**Purpose**

- Connect instrumental phrasing to the human body without prescribing one story.

### Slide 14: Contemporary music, 1950-present

**Works on this slide**

- John Williams, "Harry's Wondrous World"
- Ludwig Göransson, "Can You Hear the Music?" from *Oppenheimer*

**Historical and storytelling notes**

- Film music makes the presentation's central listening habit explicit: a recurring theme teaches the audience what a place, person, or idea feels like.
- Williams combines several themes from the first Harry Potter film into a compact portrait of its world.
- Göransson uses the violin's ability to shift quickly between beauty and instability as part of Oppenheimer's characterization.
- "Can You Hear the Music?" contains 21 tempo changes performed by live musicians. The accelerating structure turns intellectual discovery into physical momentum.

### Slide 15: Listening break, *Interstellar*

**Activity first**

- Play an excerpt without showing film footage.
- Ask the audience to describe scale, setting, relationship, and sense of time.

**Reveal second**

- Christopher Nolan gave Hans Zimmer a one-page emotional story about a parent and child before revealing the science-fiction setting.
- Zimmer developed the emotional foundation before knowing the film's cosmic scale.
- The Temple Church pipe organ became the score's defining instrument.
- This is the final proof point: an intimate human story can generate music that listeners experience as cosmic.

### Slide 16: What did you see?

**Visible content**

- A collage of stills or thumbnails associated with the excerpts and eras.
- Include Dale's personal music photos again only if repetition strengthens the closing.
- No summary of the themes.

**Closing flow**

- Return to the audience's opening assumptions about classical music.
- Ask which excerpt produced the clearest image or most surprising emotion.
- End on conversation rather than a formal conclusion.

## Presentation interaction model

### Era slides

- Every work from the era appears on the same slide.
- Use a large typographic program list, not a grid of UI cards.
- The active work may brighten or enlarge as Dale moves through the list.
- Each title has one obvious play control, a timestamp range, and an excerpt duration.
- Historical notes appear progressively or remain in speaker notes. Do not crowd the full list with paragraphs.

### Break slides

- Each break receives its own visual identity and full slide.
- Begin with the prompt before revealing the historical explanation.
- Use an embedded YouTube overlay so closing the music returns to the same slide.
- Escape closes the player. A backup YouTube link opens in a new tab.

### Navigation

- Right Arrow and Space: next slide.
- Left Arrow: previous slide.
- Escape: close media first, then open slide overview.
- Home and End: first and final slide.
- A subtle progress line and era label remain at the bottom.
- Hidden click zones support mouse navigation without adding visible buttons.
- A contents view allows direct jumps to any era or break.

## Design language for final review

### Recommended direction: living concert program

The presentation should feel like a contemporary concert program that has come to life, not a museum exhibit and not a software dashboard.

- **Background:** warm ivory rather than pure white.
- **Primary text:** near-black green, visually related to the portfolio site.
- **Era color:** one restrained accent per era, moving from stone and parchment toward richer blue, plum, red, and cinematic gold.
- **Typography:** expressive editorial serif for titles and composer names, quiet sans serif for dates, controls, and notes.
- **Imagery:** full-bleed manuscripts, architectural details, paintings, composer portraits, film stills, and Dale's personal JPGs. Favor one strong image over decorative clusters.
- **Motion:** slow crossfades, gentle image scale, and subtle staff-line or waveform movement. No bouncing controls or theatrical transitions.
- **Era slides:** one continuous vertical or diagonal program composition containing every work for that era.
- **Break slides:** simpler and more immersive, with a large listening prompt and minimal visual information.
- **Historical facts:** revealed as brief marginal notes after listening, like annotations written into a concert program.
- **Audio state:** while an excerpt plays, dim nonessential content and show the active title, elapsed time, and a clear return control.

### Implemented design decisions

- A darker, cinematic companion to the warm portfolio, using the same editorial serif and muted green family.
- Historical artwork, composer portraits, performance stills, and manuscript-like textures appear throughout.
- Every era's complete program remains visible; the selected excerpt receives emphasis.
- Audience responses stay spoken, keeping activity slides uncluttered.
- Slide text uses only short phrases; this document retains the full speaking notes.
